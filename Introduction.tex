\section{INTRODUCTION}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yber--physical} systems (CPS) integrate sensing, communication, computation, and control with physical processes. During post-attack recovery, these layers cannot always be restored sequentially. A recovery controller may have to act while observations remain partially corrupted, the communication path is delayed or replayed, and the command delivered to the actuator is perturbed. When recovery is further adapted through federated reinforcement learning (FRL), a second integrity dependency appears: the distributed knowledge used to update the recovery policy can itself be Byzantine, stale, semantically incompatible, or strategically poisoned.

This combination creates a \emph{recovery-integrity} problem. The question is no longer only whether a learned controller can recover the plant efficiently, but whether corrupted recovery knowledge can acquire enough authority to distort the controller's belief, authorize a risky action, alter the deployed policy, and finally affect the physical plant. Standard FRL does not answer this question. Data locality does not prevent model poisoning, and a robust aggregation rule that reduces optimization error does not imply that the resulting physical action is safe. Conversely, placing a safety filter after an arbitrary learned policy can protect the plant, but by itself it does not explain how malicious recovery knowledge is prevented from persistently controlling the learning and authorization process.

This paper therefore asks the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a federated recovery controller remain physically safe and retain bounded recovery authority when both the physical observation/control channels and the federated recovery knowledge are adversarially corrupted?}
\end{quote}
Our answer is conditional but explicit: yes, when physical corruption remains inside a modeled attack envelope, the uncertainty set covers the true state, the influence of corrupted federated knowledge is bounded, the belief update is contractive, online adaptation is rate-limited and gated, and the robust execution shield remains feasible. These conditions make the security boundary visible rather than hiding it inside empirical recovery accuracy.

We instantiate this principle in AEFC. Heterogeneous clients keep subsystem-specific observation and action adapters local and federate only a representation-compatible recovery backbone. However, heterogeneity handling is not the security root. The security path instead consists of four layers. First, provenance-aware temporal processing converts potentially corrupted local and federated evidence into a bounded recovery belief. Second, a risk-aware gate determines whether a learned proposal receives recovery authority. Third, trust-gated online adaptation separates candidate updates from the deployed controller and bounds the amount by which accepted evidence can move the deployed policy. Finally, a robust CBF/CLF-based execution shield enforces the hard physical safety contract before the command is delivered to the plant.

The execution order is important. Let $u_t^{\pi}$ denote the learned proposal and $u_t^\star$ the shielded command. We model control-channel corruption \emph{after} the shield and before the plant:
\begin{equation}
u_t^{\mathrm{exec}}=u_t^\star+a_t^u.
\end{equation}
This placement prevents an artificial guarantee in which the shield is allowed to ``correct'' an attack that in reality occurs downstream of the controller. Safety is therefore claimed only for $a_t^u$ inside an explicitly modeled admissible set.

The resulting architecture also clarifies the role of federated coordination. Certificate-supported local transitions, representation alignment, Top-$k$ sparsification, and Byzantine-resilient aggregation improve the quality and efficiency of distributed learning. They do not authenticate a Byzantine client and they do not replace the physical safety layer. Client-reported advantage, prototype similarity, or confidence is never treated as a trust root. The server limits Byzantine influence with a mechanism whose assumptions are stated explicitly, while the deployed controller limits the authority of accepted knowledge before physical execution.

The main contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate \emph{bounded-authority federated recovery} under joint physical-channel corruption and recovery-knowledge poisoning. The formulation separates evidence integrity, recovery authorization, online adaptation, and physical execution rather than collapsing them into one empirical robustness score.
    \item We develop a provenance-aware temporal belief update, a risk-aware recovery gate, and trust-gated online policy adaptation. Together they bound the propagation of corrupted recovery knowledge from evidence to belief, from belief to action authority, and from accepted updates to the deployed policy.
    \item We provide a compositional security analysis. The guarantees include bounded belief distortion, bounded false authorization, bounded deployed-policy deviation, finite $\varepsilon$-correction after poisoning under an explicit post-attack contraction condition, and policy-independent forward invariance under bounded post-shield control corruption.
    \item We retain heterogeneous adapters, deterministic Top-$k$ compression, and Byzantine-resilient coordination as implementation components, but revise their claims to match their actual security role. In particular, sparsification is treated as communication optimization rather than a privacy or trust mechanism.
    \item We redesign the evaluation around joint corruption, per-seed raw traces, security-specific metrics, matched statistical comparisons, and explicit theorem-boundary stress tests. Numerical claims that are not traceable to an executable simulator and raw logs are not used as evidence.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper reviews related work, presents the system and threat model and AEFC mechanisms, develops the security analysis, defines the experimental and validation methodology, and concludes with the scope and limitations of the current guarantees.

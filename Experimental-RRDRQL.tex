\section{EXPERIMENTAL}
The evaluation focuses on power-grid CPS recovery simulations and examines recovery performance, communication cost, heterogeneity stress, robustness against malicious clients, membership-inference risk, scalability, convergence diagnostics, and deployment issues. A small non-grid traffic-signal proof of concept is also included to test whether the AEFC interface depends on power-flow equations. The source code is available at https://github.com/startkz/AEFC-FRL.

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
All experiments were conducted on a workstation with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6230 CPU at 2.10~GHz, 128~GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. The software environment used Ubuntu 20.04 LTS, Python 3.9, PyTorch 2.0, and CUDA acceleration. The CPS environment was built on a Simulink--Python co-simulation platform: Simulink models the physical power-grid dynamics, and a custom Gym-compatible interface exposes states, actions, and rewards to the learning agents.

The framework is evaluated on two \emph{power-grid CPS} benchmarks: the IEEE 39-bus system and the IEEE 118-bus system. The 39-bus case is used for convergence, recovery performance, robustness, and membership-inference evaluation. The 118-bus case is used to test scalability with different numbers of agents. The current empirical evidence is therefore strongest for power-grid recovery. For other CPS domains, such as transportation control or industrial automation, the state-action semantics, actuation delay, safety constraints, and topology can differ substantially; those differences are treated as external-validity limitations rather than as already validated generalization.

The IEEE 39-bus system contains 10 generators and 39 buses and is partitioned into multiple subsystems for multi-agent control. Fig.~\ref{fig:platform} shows the co-simulation platform, where the physical network, subsystem agents, and federated coordination server are connected in one recovery pipeline. Each agent observes local measurements from its assigned subsystem and executes recovery actions under attack conditions. In federated variants, the server aggregates model updates rather than raw trajectories.

All learning methods use the same MADDPG-based actor--critic backbone. Unless otherwise stated, the actor and critic learning rates are both 0.001 with Adam, the replay buffer size is $1\times 10^5$ transitions per agent, the batch size is 256, the discount factor is 0.99, and the soft target update rate is $\tau=0.005$. Each actor and critic has two hidden layers with 128 units per layer and ReLU activations. Exploration uses Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noise with an initial scale of 0.2 that decays during training. Federated synchronization is performed every 5 episodes. For AEFC, the sparsification ratio is $\kappa=0.2$. All methods are trained for 1000 episodes with 30 steps per episode. We compare AEFC-FRL with the following baselines and ablation variants.

1) \textit{MADDPG}: a centralized learner that trains a single controller using information from the whole 39-bus system. This is an idealized full-information baseline.

2) \textit{FedRL}: a standard federated multi-agent RL baseline. Each agent trains locally and periodically uploads dense model updates for server averaging. It preserves data locality but does not use advantage-guided sparsification or credibility-aware aggregation.

3) \textit{FedRL-NoAdv}: an ablation variant without advantage execution. Clients use credibility-aware aggregation but upload dense updates, which isolates the contribution of sparse advantage-guided transmission.

4) \textit{FedRL-NoCW}: an ablation variant without critic weighting. Clients use advantage-guided sparse updates, while the server applies equal-weight aggregation.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery-time} reports recovery time over training episodes. At the beginning of training, all methods require roughly 15--18 time steps to stabilize the system after an attack. As training proceeds, recovery time decreases for all methods. AEFC-FRL reaches a stable level of about 9 recovery steps after approximately 300 episodes. MADDPG and FedRL converge more slowly and settle around 13 and 12 steps, respectively. FedRL-NoAdv and FedRL-NoCW fall between the full method and the baselines: FedRL-NoAdv plateaus at about 10--11 steps, while FedRL-NoCW eventually reaches about 9 steps but needs more training episodes. These results indicate that sparse advantage execution and credibility-aware aggregation improve both recovery speed and training stability in the studied 39-bus setting.

Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery-accuracy} shows recovery accuracy, measured by the percentage of restored load or the fraction of state variables returned to safe ranges after an attack. \subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Runtime Cost}
AEFC introduces two practical hyperparameters: the advantage threshold $\zeta$ and the sparsification ratio $\kappa$. The threshold $\zeta$ controls which local transitions are treated as high-value recovery experiences, while $\kappa$ controls the number of update coordinates transmitted to the server. A very low $\zeta$ or large $\kappa$ makes AEFC close to dense FedRL and weakens the communication benefit. A very high $\zeta$ or small $\kappa$ can discard useful recovery signals and slow policy improvement. For this reason, the default setting uses a moderate sparsification ratio $\kappa=0.2$ and selects high-advantage transitions from the upper range of the local advantage distribution.

In deployment, these parameters need not remain fixed. The advantage threshold can be set adaptively as a local quantile, e.g., selecting the top $p\%$ of recent advantage values in each client buffer. Similarly, $\kappa$ can be adjusted according to available uplink bandwidth or server-side congestion. When the channel is congested, clients can reduce $\kappa$ and upload fewer index-value pairs; when recovery performance drops or attacks intensify, $\kappa$ can be relaxed to transmit more information. This adaptive interpretation makes AEFC less dependent on hand-tuned constants and provides a practical path for bandwidth-aware operation.

AEFC also adds computation beyond standard FedRL. The main additional costs are advantage scoring, Top-$\kappa$ coordinate selection, norm clipping, and credibility-weight calculation. Advantage scores are already available from the actor--critic learner, so the dominant extra client-side operation is Top-$\kappa$ selection over the update vector. This operation is linear or near-linear in the number of parameters with standard partial-selection routines and is executed only at federation rounds rather than at every environment step. The overhead is therefore mainly a synchronization-round cost, while the saved communication volume is paid back whenever uplink bandwidth is the bottleneck. For real-time CPS, the federation interval should be chosen so that all AEFC processing finishes outside the hard control loop, with certified local controllers handling immediate safety responses.

\subsection{Heterogeneity and Shift Stress Test}
We further evaluate whether the adapter and prototype-alignment components improve stability when subsystem agents no longer share perfectly aligned observations and attack contexts. Four heterogeneity levels are considered. H0 uses aligned local observations and identical adapter dimensions. H1 masks 20\% of local sensing channels for a subset of clients. H2 combines sensing masks with communication delay and separates clients into sensor-attack and control-attack groups. H3 increases the sensing mask to 40\%, adds stochastic message delay, and applies actuator saturation to part of the system. The same federation interval, sparsification ratio, and local training budget are used across all levels.

Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-stress} reports recovery accuracy averaged over five seeds. Standard FedRL degrades sharply as heterogeneity increases, falling from about 88\% at H0 to about 64\% at H3. AEFC without local adapters performs better than FedRL but still loses accuracy when observation and action mappings become incompatible. Removing recovery-state prototypes also hurts performance under H2 and H3, showing that sparse advantage updates alone do not fully control latent drift. The full AEFC-FRL maintains about 88\% recovery accuracy at H3, which is approximately 9 percentage points higher than the no-adapter variant and 24 percentage points higher than FedRL. This stress test supports the design choice of synchronizing an aligned recovery backbone while keeping subsystem-specific adapters local.

\subsection{Robustness and Empirical Privacy-Risk Assessment}
Robustness is evaluated by injecting Byzantine clients into the federated training process at ratios of 10\%, 20\%, and 30\%. The malicious clients submit random updates or poisoned gradients, including scaled and sign-flipped perturbations. Fig.~\ref{fig:byzantine-ratio} reports the resulting recovery accuracy. As the fraction of malicious clients increases, all methods degrade, but AEFC-FRL degrades more slowly than the unenhanced federated variants. At 30\% malicious participation, AEFC-FRL preserves approximately 83\% recovery accuracy. This behavior is consistent with the design goal of AEFC: advantage-guided filtering reduces the spread of low-utility local updates, and credibility-aware aggregation lowers the weight assigned to suspicious contributions.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Fig12.eps}
	\caption{Recovery accuracy under different Byzantine-client ratios.}
	\label{fig:byzantine-ratio}
	\vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

Privacy risk is evaluated through a membership inference attack (MIA). The adversary attempts to determine whether a target sample belongs to a client's local training set. Attack success is measured by AUC, where an AUC close to 0.5 indicates performance close to random guessing under the adopted attack model. Fig.~\ref{fig:mia-auc} shows that AEFC-FRL produces the lowest AUC among the tested methods and approaches 0.5.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Fig13.eps}
	\caption{Membership inference attack AUC for each method.}
	\label{fig:mia-auc}
	\vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

A near-0.5 AUC in this experiment indicates reduced empirical membership-inference efficacy under the chosen attacker and data split. It is not a formal privacy guarantee, and it does not imply resistance to stronger inference, reconstruction, or gradient-leakage attacks. AEFC reduces the amount of transmitted information, but sparsification alone is not a substitute for differential privacy, secure aggregation, or cryptographic protection. The reported result is therefore an empirical privacy-risk assessment without formal guarantees, rather than proof of privacy preservation.

Gradient leakage is a separate threat. A gradient-reconstruction adversary may use uploaded index-value pairs, model snapshots, and prior knowledge of the subsystem dynamics to infer sensitive local states or rare attack trajectories. AEFC makes such reconstruction harder by transmitting fewer coordinates, but the nonzero coordinates are also more informative because they are selected from high-advantage transitions. In a high-assurance deployment, sparse uploads should therefore be combined with secure aggregation, update clipping, and calibrated differential-privacy noise. The expected trade-off is clear: stronger formal privacy mechanisms reduce reconstruction risk but may also attenuate the recovery signal, especially when high-advantage events are rare.

Fig.~\ref{fig:attack-intensity} complements the Byzantine analysis by examining performance degradation under three attack-intensity levels relative to the no-attack case. AEFC-FRL remains stable across the three regimes. The degradation is small under low intensity, moderate under medium intensity, and remains bounded under high intensity. The result supports the claim that sparse update transmission and credibility-aware aggregation should be used together: sparsification reduces communication and filters weak updates, while credibility weighting improves resistance to outliers.

\subsection{Scalability Evaluation}
Scalability is evaluated on the IEEE 118-bus system with 10, 20, 30, and 40 agents. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability-time}, AEFC-FRL reaches 90\% recovery accuracy in about 200--250 episodes across the tested agent counts. MADDPG and FedRL plateau below the 90\% threshold in the same training budget, while FedRL-NoAdv and FedRL-NoCW eventually exceed 90\% but require more episodes. The advantage of AEFC-FRL becomes more visible as the number of agents increases, suggesting that federated knowledge sharing is useful when the grid is partitioned into many local controllers.

\subsection{Convergence Diagnostics}
The convergence analysis in Section III relies on bounded update norm, bounded inter-client heterogeneity, controlled bias from credibility weighting, and bounded prototype drift. To make these assumptions auditable in implementation, four diagnostic quantities are logged during training: the aggregated update norm $G_t$, the relative heterogeneity estimate $H_t$, the relative credibility-weighting bias $B_t/G_t$, and the prototype-drift score $S_t$. Their theoretical definitions are given in Section III-E. Because the released actor--critic implementation exposes client parameter deltas after local optimizer steps rather than raw stochastic gradients, the logged values in Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics} use the corresponding round-level update proxies. We instrumented the released AEFC-FRL reference implementation on a CPS-like Pendulum-v1 stabilization task with five clients, 10\% random Byzantine clients, Top-$\kappa=0.2$, and three random seeds. This lightweight run is used only to audit the training assumptions; it is not reported as an additional power-grid benchmark.

These diagnostics are used as sanity checks rather than as substitutes for proof. When $G_t$ decreases together with recovery loss, the optimization process is consistent with the stationarity criterion in Eq.~\ref{eq11}. When $H_t$, $B_t/G_t$, or $S_t$ spikes, the corresponding federation round is inspected for local attack imbalance, representation drift, or abnormal client updates. This practice is useful in deployment because it exposes when the assumptions behind the convergence discussion cease to be reliable.

\subsection{External Validity and Deployment}
The power-grid benchmarks exercise important CPS properties, including continuous dynamics, coupled subsystems, safety constraints, and communication-limited distributed control. Nevertheless, they do not cover every CPS domain. To test whether AEFC is tied to the specific structure of power-flow equations, we add a small non-grid proof of concept using a two-phase traffic-signal queue simulator. Each client represents one local intersection with different arrival rates. A cross-layer incident injects sensor bias and command delay during the recovery window. The local learner uses a lightweight policy-gradient actor with a local observation encoder, and the server receives either dense FedRL updates or AEFC sparse updates selected by recovery advantage. The same interface is preserved: local observation encoding, local policy update, sparse upload, and server aggregation. Table~\ref{tab:traffic-poc} reports this proof-of-concept result.

The table uses 15 random seeds. The seed-level distribution is skewed, so the table reports both the mean queue length and the median queue with interquartile range; full mean--standard-deviation values are retained in the released CSV logs. AEFC-FRL reduced mean queue length relative to dense FedRL while using one-third fewer communication units. A dense clipped baseline performed best on this small traffic simulator, which shows that the non-grid result should be interpreted as proof of interface portability rather than as a claim of traffic-control superiority. The result supports the narrower claim that AEFC's local-encoder, sparse-upload, and aggregation interface is not hard-coded to grid topology.

The comparison with CyberForce in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} also clarifies the robustness boundary. Under 30\% malicious clients, CyberForce reports 95.0\%--98.0\% recovery accuracy, whereas AEFC-FRL preserves 83.0\%. AEFC-FRL therefore should not be read as replacing specialized Byzantine-defense mechanisms under severe adversarial participation. Its design trades some worst-case robustness for lower communication cost and recovery-conditioned sharing. In deployments where the expected malicious-client ratio is high, AEFC should be combined with stronger robust aggregation, anomaly screening, or secure execution mechanisms.

The privacy boundary is similarly important. AEFC does not include differential privacy, secure aggregation, or a cryptographic protocol. Sparse high-advantage updates can reduce the number of exposed coordinates, but the coordinates that remain may be especially informative because they are selected from rare recovery-critical transitions. Such updates may become high-value targets for gradient-reconstruction attacks, especially when an adversary has access to public model snapshots and prior knowledge of local dynamics. A high-assurance deployment should combine AEFC with secure aggregation, update clipping, calibrated differential-privacy noise, or TEE-based protection. These additions may reduce recovery signal strength, so the privacy--resilience trade-off needs to be evaluated explicitly.

The main empirical conclusion remains bounded: AEFC-FRL has been extensively validated on power-grid CPS and lightly checked on a non-grid traffic-signal simulator. Broader CPS validation still requires domain-specific simulators, safety metrics, actuation-delay models, and fallback controllers.

Practical deployment also raises engineering constraints that are not fully captured by simulation. Federated rounds require clock synchronization or an asynchronous aggregation policy when edge devices report at different rates. Edge controllers may not have enough memory or compute capacity for large actor--critic models, making model compression and local update scheduling necessary. Communication links can be intermittent, so stale updates should be timestamped, clipped, or discarded. The additional runtime cost of advantage scoring and Top-$\kappa$ selection should be scheduled outside the hard real-time control period. Finally, operational CPS often require fail-safe fallback controllers; AEFC should therefore be deployed as an adaptive recovery layer that remains subordinate to certified protection logic during unsafe transients.
